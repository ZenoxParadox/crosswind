Glossary

Plain-language definitions, written to describe each term the way its own supporters would recognise it, and to say where people use it as an insult.

First drafts. They have not yet been reviewed by people with different political views, and meanings differ by country. Examples lean on Dutch and US usage. Treat them as a starting point, not the final word.

Left and right

Two broad sides in politics, mostly about how big a role the state should play in the economy.

The words come from the French assembly after 1789, where supporters of the king sat on the right and supporters of the revolution on the left. Today, on economics, the left generally wants the state to do more to reduce inequality: higher taxes on top earners, public services, stronger rules, sometimes public ownership. The right generally wants more room for markets and personal responsibility: lower taxes, fewer rules, more private ownership.

Watch out: people also use "left" and "right" for social and cultural questions, and that is where the confusion starts. This site measures economics, social values, authority, nationalism and violence separately, so you can be left on one and right on another.

Progressive and conservative

Two attitudes toward changing society's customs and rules.

Progressive: favours changing norms and laws so that society becomes more inclusive or equal, and sees many old customs as holding people back.

Conservative: values continuity, tradition and gradual change, and sees old customs as lessons that have been tested over time.

Watch out: conservative does not have to mean religious, and progressive does not have to mean left on economics. Many people are progressive on some topics and conservative on others.

Liberal

A word that means different things in different countries.

The original meaning ("classical liberalism") is individual freedom, the rule of law, limited government and, usually, free markets. In the Netherlands and much of Europe, "liberal" (liberaal) mostly keeps that meaning: it points to personal freedom and market-friendly economics, which puts it on the centre-right for economics.

In the United States, "liberal" usually means the opposite on economics: centre-left, with progressive social views and a bigger role for government.

Watch out: the same word can describe quite different positions. Check which country's usage someone means.

Socialist

Someone who wants the economy to serve everyone through collective ownership or strong public control, to reduce inequality.

Socialism is a wide family. At the milder end, "social democrats" keep a market economy but add a strong welfare state, high taxes on high incomes and strong workers' rights. Further along, "democratic socialists" want more public or worker ownership of key industries. At the far end, communism proposes that the state or the community owns almost all production.

Watch out: in Europe the word is fairly specific. In the United States it is often used loosely, by supporters and critics alike, for almost any big-government left policy.

Libertarian

Someone who puts individual freedom first and wants the state to stay small.

On this site, "freedom first" is one end of the authority-versus-liberty scale: fewer limits on what individuals may do. Libertarians as a political movement usually also want a very small state in economics, with low taxes and few rules.

Watch out: libertarian is not the same as liberal, and a person can value personal freedom a lot while still supporting a strong welfare state.

Authoritarian

Someone who puts order and strong central authority ahead of individual freedom.

It is a spectrum. A mild form is wanting strict policing, more surveillance or tougher sentences. The extreme is a dictatorship with no real opposition, free press or independent courts.

Watch out: authoritarian describes how power is used, not left or right. There are left-wing and right-wing versions. Supporting strong policing against crime is not the same as supporting a dictatorship.

Nationalism

Putting the interests, culture and identity of your own nation first.

It covers a wide range. At the mild end it means wanting your country's interests protected and its culture preserved. At the extreme end it means believing one nation or people is superior to others.

Watch out: some people use "nationalist" proudly, others as an insult. On this site it is a scale from "cooperation first" to "country first", not a judgement.

Patriotism

Love of, and pride in, your own country.

Many people say patriotism differs from nationalism because it does not imply that other countries matter less. Others say the two overlap, or that the difference is mostly in tone.

Watch out: the nationalism question on this site asks about priorities ("my country's interests come before others"), not about pride.

Pacifism

Opposition to war and violence as a way to settle conflicts.

Strict pacifists reject all violence, including self-defense. Many people hold a milder view: violence only as a last resort, or only in self-defense or to resist an invasion.

Watch out: the "never justified" end of the political violence scale here is closest to pacifism about political violence, but it does not decide what you think about, for example, self-defense or war.

Fascism

An extreme ideology that combines intense nationalism, rule by one party or one leader, and the suppression of opposition.

It emerged in Europe in the early 20th century, with Mussolini's Italy as the original example. Typical features are a cult of the leader, rejection of liberal democracy, state control of society, and the use of violence against opponents. Historians debate the exact definition and which regimes and movements fit.

Watch out: the word is often used loosely as an insult. Being conservative, nationalist or in favour of strong policing is not the same as being a fascist. Fascism is defined by the combination of extreme features, not any single one.

Words used in the questions

  • Progressive tax: a tax where the percentage you pay rises as your income rises. Dutch income tax works this way. (Not the same as "progressive" in the political sense above.)
  • Redistribution: moving money from some groups to others through taxes and benefits.
  • Privatisation: moving ownership or running of a service from the government to private companies.
  • Regulation: rules set by the government on how companies may act, for example on prices, safety or competition.
  • Free market: an economy where prices and production are set mostly by buyers and sellers competing, with limited government interference. No economy is completely free; the question is how much.
  • Natural monopoly: a service where one provider is cheapest because building a second network (pipes, cables, rails) would be wasteful, such as the electricity grid. Competition can still exist on top of it, for example between companies selling electricity.
